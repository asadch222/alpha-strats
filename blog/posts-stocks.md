=== leveraged-etf-decay | US stocks | Leveraged ETFs: why a 3x fund does not give you 3x the long-term return
> Funds such as SOXL promise three times the daily move of an index. Over weeks and months the result can be very different, and the reason is arithmetic, not mismanagement.

## The promise is daily

A three-times leveraged fund aims to deliver three times the index's return each day. To do that it must rebalance every day so that its exposure is again three times its assets at the next open. That daily reset is the source of everything that follows.

Over a single day the fund does what it says. Over many days, returns compound, and compounding three-times daily returns is not the same as tripling the compounded return.

## A two-day example

Suppose an index rises 10% and then falls 9.09%, ending exactly where it started.

- The index: 100 → 110 → 100. Flat.
- The 3x fund: up 30% to 130, then down 27.27% to 94.5.

The index is unchanged and the fund has lost 5.5%. Nothing went wrong. The fund lost money because it had more exposure after the rise, so the following fall cost more in absolute terms than the rise had gained.

## Volatility drag

This effect is called volatility drag or decay. Its size depends on how much the index bounces around, and it grows with the square of the leverage. As a rough guide, a three-times fund suffers about nine times the drag of the unleveraged index for the same volatility.

Two conclusions follow.

- **Choppy, sideways markets are the worst case.** The index goes nowhere and the fund steadily loses value.
- **Strong, steady trends are the best case.** If the index climbs day after day with little volatility, compounding works in the fund's favour, and it can return more than three times the index.

So the outcome depends on the path, not just the destination. Two periods with the same index return can give a leveraged fund very different results.

## The inverse funds

Funds designed to return minus three times the daily move have the same structure and the same drag, plus an extra problem: markets rise over time. A long-term holder of an inverse leveraged fund is fighting both the drift and the decay. They are built for short holding periods.

## Other costs

Leveraged funds charge higher fees than plain index funds. They also pay financing costs on the borrowed exposure, which rise when interest rates rise. These are smaller than volatility drag in turbulent periods but add up in calm ones.

## Can a fund go to zero?

In principle a three-times fund would lose everything if its index fell more than a third in one day. Exchange circuit breakers that halt trading after large falls make that unlikely for broad indices, but narrow sector indices can move further. The real-world risk is less about a single day and more about a sustained decline: a fall of 50% in the index, with typical volatility along the way, can leave a three-times fund down 90% or more.

## What this means for strategies

- **Holding period matters more than direction.** Short, trend-aligned trades suit these funds. Buy-and-hold does not.
- **Trend filters earn their keep.** Because trending periods help and choppy periods hurt, rules that keep you out of sideways markets address the main weakness directly.
- **Position size must be smaller.** A fund that moves three times as much needs roughly a third of the size for the same risk.
- **Backtests need the actual fund's prices,** not three times the index, or the drag is missed.

## Key takeaways

- Leveraged funds target a multiple of the daily return, and daily compounding breaks the multiple over longer periods.
- Volatility drag grows with the square of leverage and punishes sideways markets.
- Steady trends can produce more than the stated multiple.
- Treat them as short-term trading tools and size accordingly.

=== earnings-season-playbook | US stocks | Earnings season: implied moves, reactions and what the price is really telling you
> Four times a year, companies report results and prices jump. The move is rarely about whether the numbers were good. It is about whether they were better than expected.

## Expectations are the benchmark

Before a company reports, analysts publish estimates for revenue and profit. The share price already reflects those estimates and, beyond them, an unofficial sense of what the market really expects. A company can beat the published estimate and fall, because the market expected a larger beat. It can miss and rise, because the feared outcome was worse.

So the question on results day is never "were the numbers good?" It is "were they different from what was priced?"

## The three parts of a report

1. **The past quarter.** Revenue, margins and profit against estimates.
2. **Guidance.** What management expects for the next quarter or year. This usually moves the price more than the results, because it changes forecasts.
3. **The conference call.** Tone, detail and answers to questions. Sharp reversals in the hour after a release often happen here.

## The implied move

Options prices rise ahead of earnings because traders pay for protection and for bets on a jump. From those prices you can estimate how big a move the market expects, known as the implied move. If a share has an implied move of 8%, option buyers need a larger move than that to profit, and option sellers profit if the move is smaller.

This number is useful even if you never trade options. It tells you what a normal reaction looks like for that company, so you can set stops and position sizes accordingly. Holding a full-size position through an event with an 8% expected move is a very different risk from holding it on an ordinary day.

## After the jump

Research over many decades has documented a tendency for shares to keep drifting in the direction of a strong earnings surprise for weeks afterwards. The explanation offered is that investors adjust to new information gradually. The effect is real on average but far from reliable for any single company, and it is weaker in the largest, most closely followed names.

A practical version: a share that gaps up on results and holds the gap for several days is showing that buyers are willing to pay the new price. One that gaps up and gives it all back within a day or two is showing the opposite.

## Gaps and risk

Earnings are released outside regular hours, so the price can open far from the previous close. A stop-loss does not protect against this; it fills at the open, wherever that is. The only reliable control over earnings risk is position size, or not holding through the event.

For round-the-clock stock contracts the reaction happens live in thin trading, which can exaggerate the first move before the main market opens and settles it.

## Reading across companies

Results from one company carry information about others. A chip maker's comments on demand affect its customers and competitors. A bank's loan losses say something about consumers. Early reporters in a sector often set the tone for those that follow. During earnings season, a share can move sharply on someone else's results.

## A checklist

- Know the date and time before you hold a position into it.
- Look up the implied move and compare it with your stop distance.
- Decide in advance: hold full size, reduce, or step aside.
- After the release, watch guidance and whether the gap holds.
- Do not assume a good report means a rising price.

## Key takeaways

- Prices react to the difference between results and expectations, not to the results themselves.
- Guidance usually matters more than the quarter just ended.
- The implied move sets a realistic scale for risk.
- Stops do not protect against gaps; size does.

=== sector-rotation-business-cycle | US stocks | Sector rotation: how money moves through the market as the economy changes
> Different parts of the stock market lead at different stages of the economic cycle. The pattern is imperfect but worth knowing, because sector choice often matters more than stock choice.

## Why sectors behave differently

A company that sells groceries has steady demand in good times and bad. A company that sells machinery sees orders surge in expansions and collapse in recessions. A bank earns more when rates are higher and loans are repaid. These differences mean the same economic news is good for some sectors and bad for others.

Investors try to anticipate these shifts, so share prices tend to move ahead of the economy itself, often by several months.

## The textbook cycle

**Early recovery.** The economy is turning up from a low. Interest rates are low and credit is easing. Sectors most sensitive to growth and borrowing tend to lead: consumer discretionary, financials, industrials, and smaller companies.

**Mid-cycle.** Growth is established and broad. This is usually the longest phase. Technology and industrials have often done well as companies invest. Leadership is less extreme and returns more evenly spread.

**Late cycle.** Growth slows, inflation and rates are often higher, and capacity is tight. Energy and materials have tended to hold up as commodity prices are firm. Investors begin moving toward quality and stable earnings.

**Recession.** Activity contracts. Defensive sectors with steady demand, such as consumer staples, healthcare and utilities, usually fall less. Markets typically bottom before the economy does, while the news is still bad.

## How to observe rotation rather than predict it

Predicting the cycle is hard. Observing what the market is doing is easier.

- **Relative strength.** Divide a sector fund's price by a broad index. A rising line means the sector is outperforming. This shows leadership without any forecast.
- **Cyclicals against defensives.** When economically sensitive sectors outperform defensive ones, investors are confident. When the ratio turns down, they are becoming cautious, often before the headline index shows it.
- **Small against large companies.** Smaller firms depend more on domestic growth and borrowing. Their relative performance is a read on risk appetite.

## Where the model breaks

- **Each cycle differs.** Policy, technology and shocks reshape the pattern. A cycle driven by a single dominant theme can leave one sector leading for years regardless of the stage.
- **Sectors are not uniform.** "Technology" contains stable software companies and highly cyclical chip makers. Looking one level down, at industry groups, is often more useful.
- **A few giants dominate.** In indices weighted by size, a handful of companies can account for a large share of a sector. The sector's move may be one company's move.
- **The stages are only clear afterwards.** Nobody announces that the late cycle has begun.

## Using it in practice

Use rotation as a filter for where to look. A strategy that buys pullbacks in an uptrend works better in sectors that are leading than in sectors that are lagging. A simple approach is to rank sectors by performance over the last three to six months and focus on the top few, reviewing monthly.

For traders of leveraged sector funds this matters doubly, because those funds reward sustained trends and punish sideways markets. Trading them in a sector that is leading stacks the odds; trading them in one that is drifting does the reverse.

## Key takeaways

- Sectors respond differently to growth, inflation and interest rates.
- Markets rotate ahead of the economy, not with it.
- Relative strength shows leadership without forecasting.
- Treat the cycle as a rough map and let price confirm it.

=== semiconductor-cycle | US stocks | The semiconductor cycle: why chip stocks boom and bust
> Chip shares are among the most volatile large companies in the market. The swings are not random; they follow a recognisable cycle driven by supply, demand and long factory lead times.

## An industry built on lag

Building a chip factory takes years and costs billions. Decisions to expand are made when demand is strong and prices are high. By the time the new capacity is ready, conditions have often changed. This gap between the decision and the output is the root of the cycle.

## The four phases

**Shortage.** Demand exceeds supply. Prices rise, delivery times lengthen, and customers place larger orders than they need to secure allocation. Profits surge.

**Expansion.** Encouraged by high prices, manufacturers commit to new capacity. Customers, still worried about shortages, build inventory.

**Glut.** New supply arrives as demand growth slows. Customers discover they have more stock than they need and stop ordering while they use it up. Prices fall. Orders that were double-booked during the shortage are cancelled.

**Correction.** Manufacturers cut production and spending. Inventory is worked down. Weak players struggle. Eventually supply falls below demand again and the cycle restarts.

## Why shares move before the news

Chip shares tend to peak while results are still strong and bottom while results are still terrible. Investors are pricing the next turn, not the current quarter. Record profits with inventories building are a warning. Collapsing profits with inventories falling and production cut are, historically, closer to an opportunity.

That makes the sector uncomfortable to trade. The best time to buy has usually felt like the worst.

## Indicators people watch

- **Inventory** at manufacturers and their customers, measured in days. Rising inventory with slowing sales is the classic late-cycle sign.
- **Lead times.** Lengthening means tightness; shortening means supply is catching up.
- **Capital spending plans.** Aggressive expansion announcements across the industry often cluster near the top.
- **Memory prices.** Memory chips are closest to a commodity and their prices swing the most, making them an early gauge.
- **Guidance from equipment makers.** They sell to the factories, so their orders show what manufacturers really expect.

## Not all chips are alike

- **Memory** is the most cyclical, with prices set by supply and demand almost like a raw material.
- **Processors and specialised chips** have more pricing power and steadier margins, though demand still fluctuates.
- **Equipment makers** depend on factory spending and amplify the cycle.
- **Designers without factories** carry less fixed cost but rely on a small number of manufacturers.

A sector fund blends all of these, weighted by size, which means a few very large companies drive most of its movement.

## Structural growth and the cycle

Long-term demand for chips has grown for decades as they spread into more products. New waves of demand can extend a boom far longer than usual. But structural growth does not remove the cycle. It changes its shape: longer upswings, and corrections that arrive when expectations have run ahead of even strong demand.

## Trading implications

- Volatility in the sector is high even before leverage. Size positions for it.
- Leveraged chip funds combine a cyclical sector with daily compounding. They can produce extraordinary gains in a sustained upswing and severe losses in a correction or a sideways market.
- Trend-following rules suit the sector better than buying on value, because the cycle can run much further in both directions than seems reasonable.
- Around the results of the largest companies, the whole sector moves. Check the calendar.

## Key takeaways

- Long factory lead times create repeated shortage and glut.
- Shares turn before the financial results do.
- Inventory, lead times and spending plans are the main gauges.
- High natural volatility plus leverage demands small size and a trend filter.

=== interest-rates-and-stock-valuations | US stocks | Interest rates and stock valuations: why yields move share prices
> When bond yields rise, some shares fall hard and others barely notice. The reason lies in how the value of future profits depends on the rate used to discount them.

## The basic link

A share is a claim on a company's future profits. A dollar of profit expected in ten years is worth less than a dollar today, because money available now can earn interest in the meantime. The rate used to convert future money into today's value is the discount rate, and it is built on the yield of government bonds plus an extra return for taking risk.

When yields rise, future profits are worth less today. All else equal, share prices fall. When yields fall, the opposite happens.

## Duration: why growth shares react more

The effect is not equal across companies. A firm whose profits arrive mostly in the distant future is far more sensitive to the discount rate than one earning steadily now. Borrowing a term from bonds, such shares have long duration.

- **Long duration:** fast-growing companies valued on profits years away, including much of technology.
- **Short duration:** mature companies with high current profits and dividends, such as many banks, energy firms and consumer staples.

This is why a jump in yields often hits growth shares hardest, and why falling yields tend to lift them most.

## Why the yield is moving matters

A rise in yields has different meanings.

- **Yields rising because growth is strengthening.** Profit expectations are rising too. Shares can rise alongside yields, with cyclical sectors leading.
- **Yields rising because inflation is rising.** The central bank is likely to tighten, costs go up and future profits are discounted more. This is the hostile version.
- **Yields falling because inflation is cooling.** Usually positive, especially for long-duration shares.
- **Yields falling because a recession is feared.** Profits are at risk. Shares can fall even as the discount rate eases.

Reading the cause is more important than reading the direction.

## Real yields

Part of a bond's yield compensates for expected inflation. The rest is the real yield. Because company revenues tend to rise with inflation over time, it is the real yield that matters most for valuations. A rise in real yields has been one of the more reliable headwinds for highly valued shares.

## The yield curve

Comparing short-term and long-term yields gives the yield curve. Normally long-term yields are higher. When short-term yields rise above long-term ones, the curve is inverted, which has often preceded recessions, though with long and variable delays. Banks, which borrow short and lend long, are directly affected by its shape.

## The central bank

The central bank sets the shortest rates and shapes expectations for the rest. Markets move on changes in what is expected, so a rate rise that was fully anticipated may do nothing, while a small shift in tone can move everything. Scheduled policy meetings and inflation releases are among the highest-volatility days on the calendar.

## Using this in trading

- Check the calendar for rate decisions and inflation data before taking leveraged positions.
- When yields are rising quickly, expect long-duration sectors to be weakest.
- Do not treat a single day's move as a regime. Trends in yields unfold over months.
- Remember that the relationship shifts. In some periods shares and yields rise together; in others they move opposite.

## Key takeaways

- Higher yields lower the present value of future profits.
- Growth shares are more rate-sensitive because their profits lie further ahead.
- The reason yields are moving matters more than the move itself.
- Real yields and central-bank expectations are the parts to watch.

=== market-breadth | US stocks | Market breadth: is the whole market rising, or just a few giants?
> An index can hit record highs while most of its members are falling. Breadth measures how many shares are taking part, and it often warns before the index does.

## The problem with indices

The best-known US indices weight companies by size. A handful of very large companies can therefore account for a large share of the index's movement. If those few are rising strongly, the index rises even when the typical share is flat or down. The headline number then describes the giants, not the market.

Breadth looks underneath. It asks how many shares are advancing, how many are in uptrends, and how many are reaching new highs.

## The main measures

**Advance/decline line.** Each day, subtract the number of falling shares from the number rising and add the result to a running total. A rising line means more shares are participating. If the index makes a new high while this line does not, fewer shares are doing the lifting.

**Percentage above a moving average.** The share of companies trading above their 50-day or 200-day average. Readings above roughly 80% show broad strength; below 20% show broad weakness. Extremes at both ends tend to mark turning areas.

**New highs minus new lows.** In a healthy advance, new highs comfortably exceed new lows. A rising index with expanding new lows is a sign of internal damage.

**Equal weight against size weight.** Compare an index where every company counts the same with the normal version. If the equal-weighted one lags, leadership is narrow.

## Divergence

The most useful signal is disagreement between breadth and price.

- **Negative divergence:** the index rises, breadth falls. Participation is narrowing. Rallies can continue for months this way, but they are standing on fewer legs.
- **Positive divergence:** the index makes a new low while fewer shares do. Selling pressure is fading under the surface. This has often appeared near important lows.

Divergences are warnings, not triggers. They describe condition, not timing.

## Breadth thrusts

Occasionally breadth shifts from very weak to very strong in a matter of days: nearly everything rises together. These thrusts are rare and have historically appeared near the start of sustained advances, because they show a sudden and broad return of buyers. Broad, sudden strength is a better sign than a slow recovery led by a few names.

## Why narrow markets are fragile

When a few companies carry the index, the index inherits their specific risks. One disappointing report can move the whole market. Narrow leadership also means funds tracking the index are concentrated without having chosen to be. None of this prevents further gains, but it changes the risk.

## Applying breadth

- **As a regime filter.** Strategies that buy pullbacks work better when most shares are in uptrends. If fewer than half are above their 200-day average, the odds are less favourable.
- **For leveraged index or sector funds.** These need sustained trends. Broad participation makes a trend more durable.
- **For crypto too.** The same idea applies: count how many coins are rising, not only whether Bitcoin is.
- **Alongside heatmaps.** A heatmap is breadth in picture form. A screen that is mostly one colour shows participation; a few bright tiles in a dull field shows concentration.

## Key takeaways

- Size-weighted indices can hide weakness in most shares.
- The advance/decline line, percentage above averages and new highs versus lows are the core measures.
- Divergences warn of fragility long before price turns.
- Use breadth to judge how much to trust a trend.

=== options-gamma-and-dealers | US stocks | Options, gamma and dealer hedging: the invisible hand in daily price moves
> A large share of daily stock trading comes from firms hedging options. Their mechanical buying and selling can calm markets or amplify them, depending on positioning.

## Who is on the other side

When investors buy or sell options, the counterparty is usually a dealer, a firm that makes markets and does not want a view on direction. To stay neutral, the dealer hedges by trading the underlying shares or index futures. That hedging is automatic. It follows from the mathematics of the option, not from any opinion.

## Delta and gamma in plain terms

**Delta** is how much an option's price changes when the share moves by one dollar. It tells the dealer how many shares to hold as a hedge.

**Gamma** is how quickly delta itself changes as the share moves. It tells the dealer how much the hedge must be adjusted when the price changes.

Gamma is largest for options close to the current price and close to expiry. That is where hedging activity is most intense.

## Two regimes

**Dealers long gamma.** Their hedging runs against the market: they sell as price rises and buy as it falls. This dampens moves. Markets in this state tend to be calm, with small daily ranges and reversals around popular strike prices.

**Dealers short gamma.** Their hedging runs with the market: they buy as price rises and sell as it falls. This amplifies moves. Markets in this state tend to trend within the day and produce larger ranges in both directions.

A market can flip from one state to the other as price crosses the level where dealer positioning changes sign. This is one reason a quiet market can suddenly become violent after falling through a certain level.

## Expiry effects

As options approach expiry, gamma near the current price rises, and hedging flows grow. Prices are often drawn toward strikes with a lot of open contracts, sometimes called pinning. After a large expiry, those hedges disappear, and the market is free to move more. The monthly and quarterly expiries are the largest.

The growth of options that expire within a day has concentrated these effects into individual sessions, adding intraday swings that have little to do with news.

## Volatility and the feedback loop

Hedging also responds to changes in expected volatility. When volatility falls, dealers typically buy shares to adjust; when it rises, they sell. In a calm, rising market this creates a supportive loop. In a falling market it does the reverse: declines raise volatility, which triggers selling, which deepens the decline.

## What a trader can take from this

You do not need the detailed positioning data to benefit from the concepts.

- **Expect different behaviour in calm and stressed markets.** Mean-reversion works better when ranges are small and suppressed. Trend-following works better once volatility expands.
- **Be careful around expiry dates.** Odd pinning before and freer movement after are normal.
- **Do not read meaning into every intraday swing.** Some are mechanical.
- **Watch the volatility index.** A rising reading alongside falling prices suggests the amplifying regime.

## The limits

Estimates of dealer positioning rely on assumptions about who bought and who sold each option, which are not directly observable. Published figures can be wrong. Treat them as a rough description of conditions, not a precise map of levels.

## Key takeaways

- Dealers hedge options mechanically, and that hedging moves prices.
- When dealers are long gamma, moves are dampened; when short, they are amplified.
- Expiry concentrates these effects and their removal frees the market.
- Use the idea to choose between mean-reversion and trend tactics.

=== vix-and-volatility-term-structure | US stocks | The VIX and the volatility curve: reading fear properly
> The VIX is called the fear gauge, but its level alone says little. Its relationship to recent volatility and to its own futures curve says much more.

## What the VIX is

The VIX is calculated from the prices of options on the main US index. It expresses the level of volatility those prices imply for the next 30 days, as an annual percentage. It is a measure of the price of insurance, and therefore of how much uncertainty investors are paying to protect against.

A quick conversion: divide the VIX by 16 to get the rough daily move the market is pricing. A VIX of 16 implies about 1% a day. A VIX of 32 implies about 2%.

## What it is not

It does not forecast direction. A high VIX means large moves are expected, up or down. It also is not a measure of what has happened; that is realised volatility. The two are related but distinct.

## Implied against realised

Implied volatility usually sits above what subsequently occurs. The gap is the premium earned by those selling insurance. Watching the gap is informative.

- **Implied well above realised:** investors are paying up for protection despite calm prices. Caution is high.
- **Implied close to or below realised:** protection is cheap relative to actual movement. Complacency, or a market that has not caught up with rising turbulence.

## The term structure

Futures on the VIX trade for different months. Plotting their prices gives a curve.

- **Upward sloping (contango).** Later months are priced higher than near ones. This is the normal state: calm now, uncertainty further out.
- **Downward sloping (backwardation).** Near months are priced higher. The market fears something immediate. This appears during sell-offs and is relatively rare.

A flip from the normal shape into backwardation is one of the clearer signs of genuine stress. A return to the normal shape after a sell-off suggests the acute phase is passing.

## Behaviour worth knowing

- **Volatility clusters.** Turbulent days follow turbulent days. A spike rarely vanishes overnight.
- **It reverts to its average.** Extreme readings do not persist indefinitely. Very high levels tend to fall back over weeks; very low levels tend to rise eventually.
- **It moves opposite to shares, most of the time.** Falling markets raise the VIX sharply. Rising markets lower it slowly.
- **Spikes are fast and declines are slow.** The asymmetry reflects how quickly fear arrives compared with how slowly confidence returns.

## Volatility products

Funds that track VIX futures do not track the VIX itself. In the normal upward-sloping state, such a fund must keep selling cheaper near-term futures and buying more expensive later ones, which steadily erodes its value. Leveraged versions suffer the same daily-compounding drag as any leveraged fund. They can rise enormously in a panic and lose most of their value in quiet periods. They are short-term instruments.

## Putting it to use

- **Adjust size to the regime.** When the VIX is elevated, daily ranges are larger; smaller positions keep risk constant.
- **Choose tactics by regime.** Low and stable volatility favours buying dips. Rising volatility with an inverted curve favours caution and trend-following.
- **Watch for extremes as context.** A very high reading after a long decline has often coincided with the later stages of selling. It is a condition, not a signal.
- **Check event dates.** Volatility rises into scheduled events and usually falls afterwards, whatever the outcome.

## Key takeaways

- The VIX prices expected movement, not direction.
- Compare it with realised volatility to see whether fear is rich or cheap.
- An inverted futures curve marks real stress.
- Volatility funds decay in calm markets and are not long-term holdings.

=== backtesting-pitfalls | US stocks | Backtesting pitfalls: why most strategies look better on paper than in an account
> A backtest is easy to produce and easy to get wrong. These are the errors that turn a mediocre idea into an impressive-looking chart.

## Look-ahead bias

The most common mistake is using information that would not have been available at the time. Examples:

- Acting on a candle's closing price at that same close, as though you could trade at a price you only knew once it was final.
- Using the day's high or low to decide an entry earlier that day.
- Using financial data on the date of the reporting period rather than the later date it was published.

The fix is to act only on completed information and to execute on the next available price. A backtest that enters at the next candle's open is far more believable than one that enters at the signal candle's close.

## Overfitting

Give a strategy enough adjustable settings and it can be made to fit any history. Each added rule or tuned number improves the past result and, usually, weakens the future one. The strategy has learned the noise.

Warning signs:

- Performance collapses when a setting is changed slightly.
- Many rules, each rescuing a handful of trades.
- A beautiful result from a small number of trades.
- Settings chosen after seeing which ones worked.

Robust strategies are simple and tolerant. They work reasonably across a range of settings and across several markets, not brilliantly at one precise combination.

## Out-of-sample testing

Split the history. Develop the idea on one part and test it, once, on a part it has never seen. If the unseen period is much worse, the strategy was fitted to the first. A stricter version, called walk-forward testing, repeats this in rolling steps.

The discipline only works if the unseen data stays unseen. Every time you adjust the strategy after looking at the test period, that period becomes part of the fitting.

## Survivorship bias

Testing only on companies or coins that exist today ignores those that failed and were removed. The survivors are, by definition, the ones that did well. A strategy tested on them inherits their good fortune. This matters most for long tests on shares and for almost any test on small coins.

## Costs

A test without costs is a test of a different strategy. Include:

- trading fees on every entry and exit,
- the spread and slippage, larger in thin markets and around news,
- funding or borrowing costs for leveraged and short positions,
- gaps, where a stop fills far beyond its level.

Frequent-trading strategies are the most sensitive. An edge of a fraction of a percent per trade can vanish entirely.

## Too few trades

Forty-seven trades or a five-month period can show a striking result that is largely luck. A handful of large winners often account for most of the profit. Ask how the result looks without the best three trades, and how long a losing streak is plausible at that win rate.

## Regime dependence

A strategy tested only in a rising market has been tested only for rising markets. The same applies to low-volatility periods or a single sector's boom. A test should include different conditions, or its conclusions should be limited to the conditions it covered.

## Selection bias

If you test fifty ideas and present the best one, its result overstates its quality. The more ideas tried, the more likely the winner is a coincidence. Honest reporting includes how many variations were examined.

## A believable backtest

- Signals from closed candles, entries at the next open.
- All costs included.
- Tested on unseen data and on other markets.
- Enough trades across different conditions.
- Drawdown and losing streaks reported alongside the return.

## Key takeaways

- Most backtest errors make results look better, never worse.
- Simplicity and tolerance to changed settings matter more than headline return.
- Unseen data is the only real test, and only once.
- A short test with few trades is a hypothesis, not proof.

=== position-sizing-and-risk-of-ruin | US stocks | Position sizing and risk of ruin: the maths that decides whether you survive
> Traders spend most of their time on entries. Over the long run, how much is risked on each trade matters more than when it is entered.

## The asymmetry of losses

Losses and gains are not symmetrical. After a 10% loss you need an 11% gain to recover. After 25% you need 33%. After 50% you need 100%. After 75% you need 300%. The deeper the hole, the steeper the climb.

This single fact is the argument for controlling risk. A strategy with an excellent average return can still fail if a run of losses takes the account too low to recover.

## Risk per trade

The standard method is to risk a fixed percentage of the account on each trade.

1. Choose the percentage you are willing to lose if the trade fails.
2. Find the distance from your entry to your stop.
3. Position size = (account × risk percentage) ÷ stop distance.

With a $10,000 account, 1% risk and a stop $5 away from entry, the size is 20 units. If the stop were $2 away, it would be 50 units. The loss if stopped is the same in both cases: $100.

Because the amount risked is a percentage of the current balance, position sizes shrink after losses and grow after gains. That automatic adjustment slows the damage in a losing run.

## Losing streaks are normal

With a 50% win rate, a streak of five losses will appear regularly. Over a few hundred trades, eight or more in a row is likely. With a 40% win rate, longer streaks are common.

The practical question is how much such a streak costs.

- Risking 1% per trade, ten straight losses cost about 9.6%.
- Risking 3%, about 26%.
- Risking 5%, about 40%.
- Risking 10%, about 65%.

The last two are difficult to recover from and harder still to sit through without abandoning the strategy.

## The Kelly criterion

There is a formula for the bet size that maximises long-run growth, given your win rate and the ratio of average win to average loss. It is called the Kelly criterion.

Its lesson is two-sided. Risking less than the Kelly amount grows the account more slowly. Risking more than it reduces long-run growth and, beyond roughly twice the Kelly amount, makes eventual ruin likely even with a profitable strategy.

Full Kelly is also extremely volatile and depends on knowing your true edge, which nobody does. Most professionals who use it bet a fraction, often a quarter to a half. For most traders, a fixed 0.5% to 2% per trade is a sensible range that sits well below any plausible Kelly figure.

## Leverage is an outcome, not a choice

If you size from the stop, leverage falls out of the calculation. A tight stop produces a larger position and higher leverage; a wide stop produces the opposite. Capping leverage on top of this is prudent, because gaps can carry price through a stop and make the real loss larger than planned.

## Correlated positions

Five positions each risking 1% are not 5% of independent risk if they are all technology shares or all coins. They tend to fall together. Treat highly correlated positions as one for risk purposes, and limit total exposure to a single theme.

## Drawdown rules

Decide in advance what you will do at set levels of drawdown: for example, halving risk per trade after a 10% decline, and stopping to review at 20%. Rules set while calm are more reliable than decisions made during a loss.

## Key takeaways

- Recovery requires a larger gain than the loss that caused it.
- Size each trade from a fixed risk percentage and the stop distance.
- Plan for losing streaks longer than feel reasonable.
- Risking too much destroys even a profitable strategy; smaller is safer and often faster in the long run.
