The VIX and the volatility curve: reading fear properly

The VIX is called the fear gauge, but its level alone says little. Its relationship to recent volatility and to its own futures curve says much more.

AlphaStrats Research · November 2026 · 3 min read · Advanced

What the VIX is

The VIX is calculated from the prices of options on the main US index. It expresses the level of volatility those prices imply for the next 30 days, as an annual percentage. It is a measure of the price of insurance, and therefore of how much uncertainty investors are paying to protect against.

A quick conversion: divide the VIX by 16 to get the rough daily move the market is pricing. A VIX of 16 implies about 1% a day. A VIX of 32 implies about 2%.

What it is not

It does not forecast direction. A high VIX means large moves are expected, up or down. It also is not a measure of what has happened; that is realised volatility. The two are related but distinct.

Implied against realised

Implied volatility usually sits above what subsequently occurs. The gap is the premium earned by those selling insurance. Watching the gap is informative.

  • Implied well above realised: investors are paying up for protection despite calm prices. Caution is high.
  • Implied close to or below realised: protection is cheap relative to actual movement. Complacency, or a market that has not caught up with rising turbulence.

The term structure

Futures on the VIX trade for different months. Plotting their prices gives a curve.

  • Upward sloping (contango). Later months are priced higher than near ones. This is the normal state: calm now, uncertainty further out.
  • Downward sloping (backwardation). Near months are priced higher. The market fears something immediate. This appears during sell-offs and is relatively rare.

A flip from the normal shape into backwardation is one of the clearer signs of genuine stress. A return to the normal shape after a sell-off suggests the acute phase is passing.

Behaviour worth knowing

  • Volatility clusters. Turbulent days follow turbulent days. A spike rarely vanishes overnight.
  • It reverts to its average. Extreme readings do not persist indefinitely. Very high levels tend to fall back over weeks; very low levels tend to rise eventually.
  • It moves opposite to shares, most of the time. Falling markets raise the VIX sharply. Rising markets lower it slowly.
  • Spikes are fast and declines are slow. The asymmetry reflects how quickly fear arrives compared with how slowly confidence returns.

Volatility products

Funds that track VIX futures do not track the VIX itself. In the normal upward-sloping state, such a fund must keep selling cheaper near-term futures and buying more expensive later ones, which steadily erodes its value. Leveraged versions suffer the same daily-compounding drag as any leveraged fund. They can rise enormously in a panic and lose most of their value in quiet periods. They are short-term instruments.

Putting it to use

  • Adjust size to the regime. When the VIX is elevated, daily ranges are larger; smaller positions keep risk constant.
  • Choose tactics by regime. Low and stable volatility favours buying dips. Rising volatility with an inverted curve favours caution and trend-following.
  • Watch for extremes as context. A very high reading after a long decline has often coincided with the later stages of selling. It is a condition, not a signal.
  • Check event dates. Volatility rises into scheduled events and usually falls afterwards, whatever the outcome.

Key takeaways

  • The VIX prices expected movement, not direction.
  • Compare it with realised volatility to see whether fear is rich or cheap.
  • An inverted futures curve marks real stress.
  • Volatility funds decay in calm markets and are not long-term holdings.
