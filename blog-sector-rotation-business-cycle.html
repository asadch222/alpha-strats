Sector rotation: how money moves through the market as the economy changes

Different parts of the stock market lead at different stages of the economic cycle. The pattern is imperfect but worth knowing, because sector choice often matters more than stock choice.

AlphaStrats Research · November 2026 · 3 min read · Advanced

Why sectors behave differently

A company that sells groceries has steady demand in good times and bad. A company that sells machinery sees orders surge in expansions and collapse in recessions. A bank earns more when rates are higher and loans are repaid. These differences mean the same economic news is good for some sectors and bad for others.

Investors try to anticipate these shifts, so share prices tend to move ahead of the economy itself, often by several months.

The textbook cycle

Early recovery. The economy is turning up from a low. Interest rates are low and credit is easing. Sectors most sensitive to growth and borrowing tend to lead: consumer discretionary, financials, industrials, and smaller companies.

Mid-cycle. Growth is established and broad. This is usually the longest phase. Technology and industrials have often done well as companies invest. Leadership is less extreme and returns more evenly spread.

Late cycle. Growth slows, inflation and rates are often higher, and capacity is tight. Energy and materials have tended to hold up as commodity prices are firm. Investors begin moving toward quality and stable earnings.

Recession. Activity contracts. Defensive sectors with steady demand, such as consumer staples, healthcare and utilities, usually fall less. Markets typically bottom before the economy does, while the news is still bad.

How to observe rotation rather than predict it

Predicting the cycle is hard. Observing what the market is doing is easier.

  • Relative strength. Divide a sector fund's price by a broad index. A rising line means the sector is outperforming. This shows leadership without any forecast.
  • Cyclicals against defensives. When economically sensitive sectors outperform defensive ones, investors are confident. When the ratio turns down, they are becoming cautious, often before the headline index shows it.
  • Small against large companies. Smaller firms depend more on domestic growth and borrowing. Their relative performance is a read on risk appetite.

Where the model breaks

  • Each cycle differs. Policy, technology and shocks reshape the pattern. A cycle driven by a single dominant theme can leave one sector leading for years regardless of the stage.
  • Sectors are not uniform. "Technology" contains stable software companies and highly cyclical chip makers. Looking one level down, at industry groups, is often more useful.
  • A few giants dominate. In indices weighted by size, a handful of companies can account for a large share of a sector. The sector's move may be one company's move.
  • The stages are only clear afterwards. Nobody announces that the late cycle has begun.

Using it in practice

Use rotation as a filter for where to look. A strategy that buys pullbacks in an uptrend works better in sectors that are leading than in sectors that are lagging. A simple approach is to rank sectors by performance over the last three to six months and focus on the top few, reviewing monthly.

For traders of leveraged sector funds this matters doubly, because those funds reward sustained trends and punish sideways markets. Trading them in a sector that is leading stacks the odds; trading them in one that is drifting does the reverse.

Key takeaways

  • Sectors respond differently to growth, inflation and interest rates.
  • Markets rotate ahead of the economy, not with it.
  • Relative strength shows leadership without forecasting.
  • Treat the cycle as a rough map and let price confirm it.

This article is education, not financial advice. It describes how markets tend to behave; it does not predict what any market will do. Trading involves risk of loss.
